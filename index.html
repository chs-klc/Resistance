01 / Build the idea

What does resistance mean?

Resistance tells us how much a component opposes the flow of electric current.

About 5 minutes

Resistance · 電阻

At the same voltage, a larger resistance allows a smaller current to flow.

Current is the rate of flow of electric charge. A wire does not “use up” the charge flowing through it.

UNIT OF RESISTANCEohm · Ω

Current is measured in amperes (A). Voltage is measured in volts (V).

Factor 1 · Length 長度

A longer wire has a larger resistance.

A longer wire provides a longer conducting path. Charge carriers experience more scattering along that path, so the wire offers more resistance.

In the same series circuit, a longer wire gives a smaller ammeter reading and a dimmer bulb.

Longer → more resistance → less current
Factor 2 · Thickness 粗幼

A thicker wire has a smaller resistance.

A thicker wire has a larger cross-sectional area. More charge carriers can flow through its cross-section at the same time, so the wire offers less resistance.

In the same series circuit, a thicker wire gives a larger ammeter reading and a brighter bulb.

Thicker → less resistance → more current

Make the comparison fair

Change one factor at a time. Keep the material, temperature, supply voltage and other circuit components the same. When studying length, keep thickness the same; when studying thickness, keep length the same.

✓Identify the independent, dependent and controlled variables.
✓Use an ammeter in series to compare current.
✓Use readings and a graph as evidence for your conclusion.
✓Recognise that the circuit view and diagram show the same connections.
Material and temperature also affect resistance. This lab fixes the material as nichrome and holds the temperature constant so that you can investigate length and thickness.

Investigation A: length

Predict: if the wire becomes longer, what will happen to the current?

Choose a prediction, then collect evidence.

  1. Keep the diameter, material, temperature and supply voltage the same.
  2. Close the switch. Set lengths of 30, 60, 90 and 120 cm, recording each reading.
  3. Compare the ammeter readings. Use the graph to support a conclusion.
Independent variable: length.
Dependent variable: current (ammeter reading).
Controlled: diameter, material, temperature, supply and other components.
How are the model readings calculated?

The wire is uniform nichrome. The model uses an illustrative resistivity of 1.10 Ω·mm²/m. Its cross-sectional area is πd²/4.

Rwire = ρL / A

The two cells provide an ideal 3.0 V. The bulb is represented by a fixed 4.0 Ω resistance; connecting wires, cells and ammeter are ideal.

I = 3.0 / (Rwire + 4.0)

Brightness follows the bulb’s model power, I² × 4.0. The displayed wire resistance is calculated, not measured directly by the ammeter. An actual filament bulb changes resistance as it warms, and real readings include heating, cell and contact effects.

The qualitative relationships are the Junior Science focus. The resistivity equation is an optional extension.

03 / Check your thinking

Six ideas to reconsider

Use circuit evidence to challenge these common mistakes.

Misconception

“A thicker wire has more material, so its resistance is larger.”

Correct idea

A thicker wire has a larger cross-sectional area and a smaller resistance when length, material and temperature are the same. More charge can flow through the cross-section at once.

Misconception

“A longer wire makes a larger current because it contains more electrons.”

Correct idea

A longer wire has a larger resistance. With the same voltage and other components, the current is smaller. Current is the rate of charge flow, not the total number of electrons in the wire.

Misconception

“Current gets used up as it travels through the wire.”

Correct idea

In a steady series circuit, the current is the same at every point. Electrical energy is transferred to the bulb and wire; electric charge is conserved.

Misconception

“A dimmer bulb must mean a larger wire resistance.”

Correct idea

This conclusion needs a fair comparison: the same supply, bulb and other components. A weaker cell could also make a bulb dimmer. Use the ammeter to compare current.

Misconception

“Opening the switch makes the wire’s resistance zero.”

Correct idea

Opening the switch breaks the conducting path, so the current is zero. The wire’s own resistance still depends on its length, thickness, material and temperature.

Misconception

“Doubling wire diameter halves the resistance.”

Optional extension

Cross-sectional area depends on diameter squared. Doubling the diameter gives four times the area, so the wire resistance becomes one quarter if the other factors are unchanged. The whole circuit also contains the bulb, so its current does not simply become four times as large.

*Use the same supply voltage, bulb and other components in the same series circuit.

Explain with evidence

“As wire length increases, the current decreases.”

The same voltage drives a smaller current because the wire’s resistance is larger. The bulb becomes dimmer.

Explain with evidence

“As wire thickness increases, the current increases.”

The same voltage drives a larger current because the wire’s resistance is smaller. The bulb becomes brighter.

A fair-test checklist

  • Change only length or thickness.
  • Keep the wire material and temperature the same.
  • Keep supply voltage and other circuit components the same.
  • Connect the ammeter in series.
  • Record several readings, look for a pattern and draw a conclusion.

In a real school experiment, use a low-voltage supply and open the switch between readings to reduce heating.
